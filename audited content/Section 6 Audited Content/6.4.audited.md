# Alienation Clause and Due-on-Sale Clause in Mortgages

The **Alienation Clause** operates within **mortgages** and **deeds of trust** as a lender protection mechanism.

Example: When a borrower takes a loan to purchase property, this clause protects the lender's interests.

## What is the Alienation Clause?

The Alienation Clause grants lenders the right to demand full debt payment when borrowers transfer ownership, title, or any property interest. This provision is commonly known as the **due-on-sale clause**.

## Impact on Property Sales and Loan Assumption

- When selling mortgaged property, new buyers cannot assume the existing loan without lender approval.
- This clause prevents loans from being **assumable** by new buyers without lender consent.

## Federal Law and Due-on-Sale Clauses

- Federal law generally allows due-on-sale clauses.
- The Garn-St. Germain Act of 1982 allows lenders to enforce these clauses in most situations.
- There are limited exceptions where due-on-sale clauses cannot be enforced, such as transfers to family members or into certain trusts.
- Lenders can generally enforce due-on-sale clauses without proving threat to security interest.

## Alienation Clause Applications

The Alienation Clause affects the relationship between **lenders**, **borrowers**, and **property ownership transferability**.
