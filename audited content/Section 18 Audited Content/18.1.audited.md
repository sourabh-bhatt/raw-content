# Title Searches and the Role of the Abstractor

<!-- A **title search** is done by a detective of sorts, specifically known as an **abstractor** or **title examiner**. A **title search** is conducted by an **abstractor** or **title examiner**. The role involves searching **public records** to determine if a property's title is **marketable** - suitable for **title insurance**. This process creates a **title abstract**.

## Role in Property Transactions

<!-- Before a property sale, the seller and buyer need to agree on the title status. They usually use the **abstract of title**, or **title commitment**, as a common ground for agreement. -->

Before property sales, sellers and buyers need to agree on title status. The **abstract of title**, or **title commitment**, provides common ground for agreement.

## The Title Search Process

- Often, an abstractor will conduct **two title searches**:
  1. The initial search before closing.
  2. A follow-up search after the deal is closed.

- The follow-up search may involve obtaining an **affidavit of title** from the seller. This document confirms that no new judgments, bankruptcies, divorces, or other events have occurred that could affect the property since the last title examination.

## Key Sources in Public Records

When examining public records, key sources include:

- **Grantor-Grantee Index**: A record book listing deeds, showing the transfer of property from one party (grantor) to another (grantee).
- **Will Index**: A record of wills that may affect property ownership.

## Timeline for Title Examination

<!-- As a title examiner, you must adhere to a timeline for your search. Virginia requires looking back at least **60 years** into public records from the date of your examination. However, this duration can vary depending on state laws. -->

Title examiners must adhere to specific search timelines. Virginia requires looking back at least **60 years** into public records from the examination date. This duration can vary depending on state laws.
