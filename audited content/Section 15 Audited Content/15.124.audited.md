# Understanding Misrepresentation and Fraud in Real Estate

<!-- When you step into the world of real estate, understanding the differences between **uninformed misrepresentation** and **deliberate misrepresentation**â€”or **fraud**â€”is crucial. Let's break down these concepts. -->

In real estate, understanding the differences between **uninformed misrepresentation** and **deliberate misrepresentation** - or **fraud** - matters for professional practice.

## Uninformed Misrepresentation

<!-- Uninformed misrepresentation happens when you, as the agent, incorrectly present a fact believing it to be true, due to negligence or a lack of information. Even if made without ill intent, this kind of error can lead to serious consequences, such as making a contract void or voidable depending on the significance of the misrepresentation. -->

Uninformed misrepresentation happens when agents incorrectly present a fact believing it to be true, due to negligence or lack of information. This involves stating something as a fact when, with proper diligence, the agent should or could have known otherwise. Even if made without ill intent, this error can lead to serious consequences, such as making a contract void or voidable depending on the significance of the misrepresentation.

Example:

- You assist a client interested in buying a property to open a doggie daycare.
- You find a property in a zone where a child daycare operates and assume, without verifying, that it would also allow for a doggie daycare.
- If your client proceeds with the purchase and later discovers zoning laws prohibit their business plan, your actions - though unintentional - could be viewed as negligent misrepresentation.

<!-- This is because you could haveâ€”and should haveâ€”verified the zoning laws before advising your client. -->

This occurs because the agent could have - and should have - verified the zoning laws before advising the client.

## Deliberate Misrepresentation (Fraud)

Deliberate misrepresentation involves intentionally sharing incorrect information, knowing it to be false, and expecting the other party to rely on it. This level of deception undermines trust and can lead to legal repercussions, including potential court-ordered damages and contracts being declared void or voidable.

Example:

- In the previous scenario, if you were aware through your research that doggie daycares were explicitly excluded by zoning laws but told your client the opposite to secure the sale, this would constitute fraud.
