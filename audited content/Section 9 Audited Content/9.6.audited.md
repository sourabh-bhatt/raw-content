# Understanding Liens and Foreclosure in Mortgage Properties

The concept of **liens** in **mortgaged properties** affects foreclosure processes that occur when a borrower defaults on their loan. Different states use different methods, including **judicial foreclosure** and **strict foreclosure**.

<!-- **Important Note for Virginia:** Virginia is a **title theory state** that primarily uses **deed of trust** with non-judicial foreclosure. However, understanding these other foreclosure methods helps you work with clients from other states. -->
**Note for Virginia:** Virginia is a **title theory state** that primarily uses **deed of trust** with non-judicial foreclosure. These other foreclosure methods apply when working with clients from other states.

## Judicial Foreclosure

Judicial foreclosure is a process used in some lien theory states when a borrower cannot meet their mortgage obligations. Here's what happens:

- To start a judicial foreclosure, lenders must file a lawsuit against the borrower.
- The lawsuit outlines the specifics of the debt and the mortgage in question and proves the default.
- Both parties can present their cases: the lender argues the default, and the borrower can contest the claim.
- If the lender convinces the court, a **judgment for foreclosure** is issued.
- This judgment leads to the property being auctioned off publicly after a legally required advertising period.

**Note:** Judicial foreclosures can be time-consuming and costly. This is not the primary foreclosure method used in Virginia.

## Right of Redemption

The **right of redemption** exists in some states after a foreclosure sale:

- The original owner may have a grace period to reclaim their property.
- This period varies from one month to over a year depending on state laws.
- During this time, the auction's highest bidder holds a **certificate of sale** but does not yet own the property.
- If the original owner fails to redeem the property within this window, the bidder gets the deed.

This period is called the **equity of redemption** or **statutory right of redemption**.

**Virginia Note:** Virginia has very limited redemption rights compared to other states.

## Strict Foreclosure

Strict foreclosure is used in some states when a borrower defaults:

- Proper notice must be served to the borrower and legal documents must be filed.
- Instead of a public auction, the court sets a deadline for the borrower to pay what they owe.
- If the borrower doesn't meet this deadline, the lender gets full ownership of the property directly from the court.
- The borrower loses their equity and any right to reclaim the property.

**Note:** Strict foreclosure is not commonly used in Virginia.
