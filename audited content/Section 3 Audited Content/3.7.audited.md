# Understanding Easements in Property Rights

In **property rights**, an **easement** is a limited right to use another person’s land for a specific purpose. It may be granted voluntarily by the landowner or created by law. Granting an easement reduces the landowner’s rights and can affect how the property is used.

## Types of Easements

Let's break easements down into two types:

- **Easements Appurtenant**: Connected to the ownership of specific real estate. It's like a property accessory that only goes with the one it was designed for.
- **Easements in Gross**: Attached to an individual, regardless of the property they own. Think of it as a personalized access card.

## Who Can Hold Easements?

Both individuals and the government can hold easements. These rights can come into existence in various ways:

- Through **mutual agreements**
- By someone using the land in a certain way without permission (known as **adverse and hostile use**)
- Government intervention

## Easements and Property Transfer

Some easements are tied to the land itself rather than to the person who made the agreement. This means if a piece of real estate is sold, the new owner could inherit an existing easement.

## Ways Easements Are Created

Easements can be established in several ways:

1. **Expressly stated** (explicit agreement)
2. **Implied** easements
3. Easements created **out of necessity**
4. Easements recognized through long-term use (**prescription**)
5. Officially taken for public use (**condemnation**)

## Easements vs. Licenses

Don't confuse an easement with a **license**:

- A license is a personal, temporary pass to use someone's property.
- It is less formal and can be revoked at any time.
- It does not transfer with the property if sold.
